=== intro ===
Yesterday's Last Stone Weight used a heap holding **everything**, and asked it for the maximum over and over. Today the heap gets smaller - deliberately. You will keep only `k` elements, and the surprise is *which kind* of heap does it.

> To find the k-th largest, do not track the largest. Track the **weakest member of the top k**. The element at the door of the club, not the one at the head of it, is the one the answer is about.

=== problem ===
Given an integer array `nums` and an integer `k`, return the **k-th largest element** in the array. It is the k-th largest in sorted order, **not** the k-th distinct element. The problem asks you to try to solve it without sorting.

```text
nums = [3, 2, 1, 5, 6, 4],  k = 2   ->  5
nums = [3, 2, 3, 1, 2, 4, 5, 5, 6],  k = 4   ->  4
```

### Reading the second example

Sort it from largest to smallest and count:

| rank | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 |
| :--- | -: | -: | -: | -: | -: | -: | -: | -: | -: |
| value | 6 | 5 | 5 | **4** | 3 | 3 | 2 | 2 | 1 |

The two `5`s occupy ranks 2 **and** 3. Duplicates count separately - that is what "not necessarily distinct" means. If they were collapsed, rank 4 would be `3`, which is wrong.

This is the example we dry-run in every section: **`[3, 2, 3, 1, 2, 4, 5, 5, 6]`, `k = 4`, answer `4`.**

### Constraints

- `1 <= k <= nums.length <= 10^5`
- `-10^4 <= nums[i] <= 10^4`

`k` is always valid, so there is always an answer. `n` up to `10^5` means `O(n log n)` passes; the question is whether you can do better, and what "better" depends on.

=== pattern ===
**Min heap of size k.** (Also: Quickselect, as the follow-up.)

The reframe: the k-th largest is the **smallest of the k largest**. So if you could maintain "the k largest elements seen so far" as you scan, the answer would be the minimum of that group.

Now ask which operations that group needs:

- **Look at its minimum** - that is the current candidate answer, and the bar a new element must beat.
- **Remove its minimum** - when a bigger element arrives and bumps the weakest member out.
- **Insert** - the new element joining.

"Peek min, remove min, insert" is a **min heap**. That is why the k *largest* uses a *min* heap - the heap is pointed at the weakest member, because the weakest member is the one that ever changes.

### The club picture

```text
the top-4 club so far:   { 2, 3, 3, 4 }       min heap, top = 2
                             ^
                        doorkeeper: the weakest member

new element 5 arrives:   5 > 2  -> 5 joins, 2 is thrown out
club becomes:            { 3, 3, 4, 5 }       top = 3
```

Once the scan ends, the doorkeeper is the k-th largest.

### Recognising it

| You need | Keep | Because the heap's top is |
| :------- | :--- | :------------------------ |
| k largest / k-th largest | **min** heap of size `k` | the weakest of the top k |
| k smallest / k-th smallest | **max** heap of size `k` | the strongest of the bottom k |

The mental question:

> Which element of my "best k" is the one that gets replaced when something better arrives? Put **that** at the top of the heap.

=== bruteForce ===
The definition says "k-th largest in sorted order", so sort and read it off. After an ascending sort, the largest is at index `n - 1`, the second largest at `n - 2`, and the k-th largest at `n - k`.

### Dry run

```text
nums = [3, 2, 3, 1, 2, 4, 5, 5, 6],  k = 4,  n = 9
after Arrays.sort:  [1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4, 5, 5, 6]
```

Reading the sorted array from the right, rank by rank:

| index i | sorted[i] | rank from top (`n - i`) | rank == k? |
| ------: | --------: | ----------------------: | :--------- |
| 8 | 6 | 1 | no |
| 7 | 5 | 2 | no |
| 6 | 5 | 3 | no |
| 5 | 4 | 4 | **yes** |

The code jumps straight to `n - k = 9 - 4 = 5`, which is exactly the row where the rank reaches `k`. Answer `4`. Correct.

### Java

```java
class Solution {
    public int findKthLargest(int[] nums, int k) {

        Arrays.sort(nums);                // ascending
        return nums[nums.length - k];     // k-th from the right
    }
}
```

### Complexity

**Time:** `O(n log n)` - **Space:** `O(log n)` for the sort's recursion (Java sorts `int[]` with dual-pivot quicksort, in place).

### Where it hurts

Look at what the sort produced, and what we actually used:

```text
sorted:  [ 1, 2, 2, 3, 3 | 4 | 5, 5, 6 ]
           \___________/  ^   \_____/
           order wasted   |   order wasted
                        used
```

- The five elements left of the answer ended up in perfect order. We only needed to know they are **smaller** than the answer, not how they rank among themselves.
- The three elements right of it ended up in perfect order too. We only needed to know they are **larger** - not which of `5, 5, 6` is biggest.

Sorting computes a full ranking of all `n` elements and we read one position from it. That full ranking is what costs `log n` per element. When `k` is much smaller than `n` - "the 10th largest of a million" - almost all of that work is thrown away.

So the question is:

> Can I keep only the k largest elements as I scan, and never spend effort ordering the rest?

=== optimization ===
### The key observation

To know the k-th largest at the end, you only need the k largest elements. Everything else can be discarded **the moment it is known not to be in the top k**. And the cheapest member of the top k to compare against is the smallest one.

### The algorithm

Scan left to right. For each number:

1. `offer` it into a min heap.
2. If the heap now has `k + 1` elements, `poll` - throw out the smallest, which provably cannot be in the top k (k elements are bigger than or equal to it).

When the scan ends, the heap holds exactly the k largest, and `peek()` is the smallest of them: the k-th largest.

### Dry run on the same example

`k = 4`. The heap column shows its contents **sorted for readability**; internally it is in heap order, and only the front is guaranteed to be the minimum.

| step | num | heap after offer | size > k? | polled | heap after | top |
| ---: | --: | :--------------- | :-------- | -----: | :--------- | --: |
| 1 | 3 | `{3}` | no | - | `{3}` | 3 |
| 2 | 2 | `{2, 3}` | no | - | `{2, 3}` | 2 |
| 3 | 3 | `{2, 3, 3}` | no | - | `{2, 3, 3}` | 2 |
| 4 | 1 | `{1, 2, 3, 3}` | no | - | `{1, 2, 3, 3}` | 1 |
| 5 | 2 | `{1, 2, 2, 3, 3}` | **yes** | 1 | `{2, 2, 3, 3}` | 2 |
| 6 | 4 | `{2, 2, 3, 3, 4}` | **yes** | 2 | `{2, 3, 3, 4}` | 2 |
| 7 | 5 | `{2, 3, 3, 4, 5}` | **yes** | 2 | `{3, 3, 4, 5}` | 3 |
| 8 | 5 | `{3, 3, 4, 5, 5}` | **yes** | 3 | `{3, 4, 5, 5}` | 3 |
| 9 | 6 | `{3, 4, 5, 5, 6}` | **yes** | 3 | `{4, 5, 5, 6}` | 4 |

`peek()` returns `4`. Correct.

### Reading the table

- **Steps 1-4 fill the club.** Until there are `k` elements, nothing can be ruled out.
- **Step 5** evicts the `1`: four elements (`2, 2, 3, 3`) are at least as large, so `1` can never be 4th largest.
- **Steps 7-9: the top only ever rises.** `2 -> 3 -> 4`. The doorkeeper gets stricter as better elements arrive, which is why the final top is the answer.
- **Duplicates need nothing special.** Both `5`s stay in the heap as separate entries, exactly as "not distinct" requires.

### The follow-up: Quickselect

Quickselect is quicksort that only recurses into **one** side. Partition around a pivot; you then know the pivot's final sorted position. If that is the target index `n - k`, done. If the target is left of it, repeat on the left part only; otherwise the right part only.

On average each partition halves the range: `n + n/2 + n/4 + ... = 2n`, so **`O(n)` average**. But a consistently bad pivot (always the smallest) shrinks the range by one each time: **`O(n^2)` worst case**. A random pivot makes that astronomically unlikely, and a three-way partition (`<`, `==`, `>`) stops arrays full of duplicates from triggering it. The full code is in the implementation section.

=== complexity ===
### Time

`n` offers and at most `n - k` polls, each on a heap of at most `k + 1` elements, so each costs `O(log k)`.

```text
O(n log k)
```

### Space

The heap never holds more than `k + 1` elements.

```text
O(k)
```

### Comparison

| Approach | Time | Extra space | Notes |
| :------- | ---: | ----------: | :---- |
| Sort, index `n - k` | `O(n log n)` | `O(log n)` | modifies the input |
| Max heap of all `n`, poll `k` times | `O(n + k log n)` | `O(n)` | heapify is `O(n)` |
| **Min heap of size k** | **`O(n log k)`** | **`O(k)`** | works on a stream, input untouched |
| Quickselect (random, 3-way) | `O(n)` avg, `O(n^2)` worst | `O(1)` | fastest in practice, modifies the input |

### When each one wins

- `k` small relative to `n`, or the data arrives as a **stream** you cannot store: the size-k heap. It never needs more than `k` elements in memory and gives the current answer at every point of the scan.
- One-off query on an array you are allowed to rearrange: Quickselect.
- `k = n / 2` (the median): `log k` is `log n`, so the heap is no better than sorting - use Quickselect.

=== implementation ===
```java
class Solution {
    public int findKthLargest(int[] nums, int k) {

        // MIN heap holding the k largest values seen so far.
        // Its top is the weakest of them: the k-th largest.
        PriorityQueue<Integer> heap = new PriorityQueue<>();

        for (int num : nums) {
            heap.offer(num);
            if (heap.size() > k) {
                heap.poll();        // the smallest cannot be in the top k
            }
        }

        return heap.peek();
    }
}
```

### The lines that matter

**1.**

```java
PriorityQueue<Integer> heap = new PriorityQueue<>();
```

The default Java `PriorityQueue` - a **min** heap. For the k largest this is exactly what you want, with no comparator. Writing `Collections.reverseOrder()` here out of habit from yesterday is the classic mistake: a max heap of size k evicts the *largest* elements and ends up holding the k smallest.

**2.**

```java
if (heap.size() > k) {
    heap.poll();        // the smallest cannot be in the top k
}
```

Offer first, then trim. The new element always gets a chance; if it is smaller than everything in the heap, it simply becomes the top and is polled straight back out. That is why no separate "is it bigger than the top?" comparison is needed.

**3.**

```java
return heap.peek();
```

After the loop the heap holds exactly `k` elements (`k <= n` is guaranteed), and the top is the smallest of the k largest.

### Quickselect, for the follow-up

```java
class Solution {
    private final Random rand = new Random();

    public int findKthLargest(int[] nums, int k) {

        int target = nums.length - k;   // the answer's index in ascending order
        int lo = 0, hi = nums.length - 1;

        while (true) {
            int pivot = nums[lo + rand.nextInt(hi - lo + 1)];

            // Three-way partition of nums[lo..hi]:  < pivot | == pivot | > pivot
            int lt = lo, i = lo, gt = hi;
            while (i <= gt) {
                if (nums[i] < pivot) {
                    swap(nums, lt++, i++);
                } else if (nums[i] > pivot) {
                    swap(nums, i, gt--);
                } else {
                    i++;
                }
            }

            // Now nums[lt..gt] all equal pivot and sit in their final sorted positions.
            if (target < lt) {
                hi = lt - 1;
            } else if (target > gt) {
                lo = gt + 1;
            } else {
                return pivot;
            }
        }
    }

    private void swap(int[] a, int i, int j) {
        int t = a[i];
        a[i] = a[j];
        a[j] = t;
    }
}
```

The `== pivot` band matters. With a plain two-way partition, an array of `10^5` identical values puts the pivot at one end every time and degrades to `O(n^2)`. The three-way version puts the whole band in place in one pass and returns immediately.

### Java notes

- `heap.peek()` returns an `Integer`, auto-unboxed to `int` on return. It is never `null` here because the heap always holds `k >= 1` elements by the end.
- `Arrays.sort(int[])` in the brute force is in place and **changes the caller's array**. LeetCode does not mind; a real API usually would. The heap version leaves `nums` untouched.
- The Quickselect `Random` is a field, not created per call, and unseeded on purpose: a fixed seed would let a crafted input hit the worst case every time.

=== edgeCases ===
### k = 1 - the maximum

```text
[3, 2, 1, 5, 6, 4],  k = 1  ->  6
```

The heap holds one element, and every offer of something larger evicts the old one. It degenerates into "track the max".

### k = n - the minimum

```text
[3, 2, 1, 5, 6, 4],  k = 6  ->  1
```

The heap never exceeds `k`, so nothing is ever polled. It ends holding everything, and the top is the overall minimum.

### Duplicates count separately

```text
[3, 2, 3, 1, 2, 4, 5, 5, 6],  k = 4  ->  4
[3, 3, 2],  k = 2  ->  3
```

In the second case the two largest are `3` and `3`, so the 2nd largest is `3`, not `2`. Anything that deduplicates first (a `TreeSet`, a `HashSet`) gets this wrong.

### All equal

```text
[7, 7, 7],  k = 2  ->  7
```

Every rank holds the same value. This is also the input that breaks a two-way-partition Quickselect at scale; the three-way version returns on the first partition.

### Negative numbers

```text
[-1, -5, -3],  k = 2  ->  -3
```

Order is order. Descending: `-1, -3, -5`. Nothing changes for negatives.

### Single element

```text
[1],  k = 1  ->  1
```

### The wrong-heap trap

```text
[3, 2, 1, 5, 6, 4],  k = 2,  using a MAX heap of size 2  ->  2
```

A max heap evicts its largest element whenever it overflows, so it ends up keeping the two **smallest** (`1, 2`) and its top is `2` - the 2nd *smallest*. Wrong answer, and nothing crashes.

=== takeaway ===
> When you see **"k-th largest"** or **"top k"**, think **min heap of size k**. For "k-th smallest" or "bottom k", flip it: max heap of size k. The heap's top is always the member of the group that is next to be replaced.

### The core observation

The k-th largest is the smallest of the k largest. Keep only those k; anything smaller than their minimum can be thrown away the moment it is seen. Holding `k` elements instead of `n` turns each operation from `log n` into `log k`.

### The pattern trigger

```text
          "k-th largest" / "top k largest"
                        |
                        v
        keep the k largest seen so far
                        |
       who gets replaced when a bigger one arrives?
                        |
                        v
              the SMALLEST of them
                        |
                        v
     MIN heap, size k:  offer, then poll if size > k
                        |
                        v
            answer = heap.peek()
```

### The common mistakes

**Max heap for "largest".** It feels natural and is backwards. A max heap of size k keeps the k smallest.

**Deduplicating.** "k-th largest element" counts duplicates. `[3, 3, 2]` with `k = 2` is `3`.

**Two-way Quickselect on duplicate-heavy input.** Correct on small tests, `O(n^2)` on `10^5` copies of one value.

**Claiming Quickselect is `O(n)`** without saying "average". Its worst case is `O(n^2)`; an interviewer will ask.

### What to remember in six months

> Largest -> min heap. Smallest -> max heap. The heap points at the door, not the throne.
